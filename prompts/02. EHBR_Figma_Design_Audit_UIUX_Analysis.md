# EHBR — Comprehensive Figma Design and User-Flow Audit

## Role

Act as a senior UI/UX designer, UX researcher, information architecture specialist, accessibility reviewer, and design systems analyst.

Your task is to inspect and audit the existing Emergency Hub Barangay Rizal (EHBR) Figma design.

**Figma:**
https://www.figma.com/design/u5Q8OUeEVo8fVRbEV9OfKh/EHBR--Emergency-Hub-Barangay-Rizal----UI-UX-Prototype?node-id=4-305&p=f&t=tvFcRq2X4YmCX2GC-0 


**Primary objective:** Establish a reliable inventory of the design, understand the intended resident and administrator experiences, identify design gaps and inconsistencies, and produce a prioritized design-improvement report that can later be compared against the actual codebase.

This is a Figma design audit. Do not claim to know which functions are implemented in code unless that evidence is separately available and explicitly inspected.

## 1. Inspect the Connected Figma File

Use the available Figma MCP tools to examine the connected EHBR design file.

Inspect the accessible:
- Pages and sections.
- Frames and nested screens.
- Components and component variants.
- Shared styles and design tokens, where available.
- Prototype connections and destinations.
- Relevant responsive or alternate-device frames.
- Supporting illustrations, icons, and other design assets.

Identify the Resident Mobile App (MAM) and Admin Web App (WAM) areas.

Inspect the design in manageable batches, maintaining an inventory so you do not repeatedly analyze the same frames or skip large areas.

At the beginning of the report, state:
- The file and pages actually accessed.
- The relevant frame or component coverage.
- Whether prototype connections were inspected.
- Any inaccessible pages, hidden areas, or tool limitations.

Never claim that every screen was inspected unless the actual access and coverage support that claim.

## 2. Frame and Component Inventory

Create a structured inventory of the relevant screens and components.

For each frame, record:
- Exact frame name.
- Application mode: MAM or WAM.
- Main purpose.
- Intended user.
- Primary and secondary actions.
- Important UI components.
- Relevant navigation destinations.
- Related screens or workflow.
- Design completeness and observed issues.

Identify potential duplicates, outdated variants, disconnected frames, inconsistent names, and unexplained design differences. Compare relevant frames before labeling anything a duplicate or obsolete.

Separate confirmed observations from likely interpretations.

## 3. Resident Mobile App (MAM)

Inspect the complete accessible resident experience, including relevant screens for:

- Splash and onboarding.
- Login and account registration.
- Account verification and recovery.
- Home or dashboard.
- Emergency SOS.
- Create-report and hazard-reporting forms.
- Report submission confirmation.
- Offline or queued reports.
- My Reports, report details, and status tracking.
- Maps, incident locations, and nearby reports.
- Emergency contacts and details.
- News, announcements, and article details.
- Guided routes and emergency checklists.
- Profile, account details, address or zone, preferences, privacy, and FAQs.
- Location, camera, and notification permission explanations, where applicable.

Do not assume all these screens are present. Search the accessible design before assigning a status.

For each represented workflow, evaluate:
- Entry point and navigation clarity.
- Primary action visibility.
- Form labels, validation guidance, and required fields.
- Loading, success, failure, offline, and empty states where applicable.
- Confirmation and error recovery.
- Back navigation and ways to resume a task.
- Consistency of labels, statuses, and information.
- Suitability for residents using a small screen or operating under stress.

Identify missing states and incomplete journeys without assuming unshown behavior.

## 4. Admin Web App (WAM)

Inspect all accessible administrative screens, including relevant designs for:

- Admin login.
- Dashboard.
- Incident map and monitoring.
- Report listings and report details.
- Report review and status-management interactions.
- Records or database-management areas.
- Settings and account-management areas.
- Navigation sidebar, header, tables, filters, search, pagination, forms, dialogs, and notifications.

Evaluate whether information hierarchy and navigation support efficient administrative work.

Pay attention to how urgent incidents are distinguished from routine items, how information is grouped, and whether actions and their consequences are clear.

Identify missing screens, inconsistent layouts, unclear status representations, and incomplete user journeys. Distinguish confirmed omissions from areas that could not be inspected.

## 5. User Flows and Information Architecture

Inspect actual prototype connections where accessible.

Document the ordered screen sequence for the major resident workflows, such as:
- Registration and login.
- Submitting a report and seeing confirmation.
- Tracking a report.
- Triggering an SOS action and its confirmation.
- Locating an incident.
- Accessing contacts and announcements.
- Managing account settings.

Document the major administrator workflows, such as:
- Admin login.
- Finding an incident report.
- Reviewing report details.
- Performing available review or status actions.
- Monitoring incident locations.
- Accessing administrative records and settings.

For each flow, identify:
- Entry point.
- Screen sequence.
- Primary action on each screen.
- Expected destination.
- Missing or disconnected destinations.
- Dead ends and confusing transitions.
- States needed for recovery or interruption.

If the prototype wiring is inaccessible, describe the apparent flow as inferred rather than verified.

Provide a text-based flow map that can later guide implementation and testing.

## 6. Visual Design System

Inspect the consistency and usability of the design system across MAM and WAM.

### Color
Review palette consistency, semantic use, contrast, and distinctions among emergency, warning, success, and error states.

### Typography
Review font styles, heading hierarchy, text sizing, line spacing, readability, and consistency.

### Spacing and layout
Review alignment, padding, spacing patterns, grid structure, grouping, content density, and screen balance.

### Components
Inspect buttons, input fields, dropdowns, cards, navigation, tables, dialogs, status indicators, and recurring patterns. Identify inconsistent variants and opportunities for component reuse.

### Iconography and imagery
Check meaning, sizing, alignment, visual consistency, and appropriateness.

### Copy and terminology
Identify spelling errors, unclear labels, inconsistent terminology, placeholder text, and inconsistent capitalization.

Reference the actual frame or component name whenever possible. Explain the user impact of every important recommendation.

## 7. Responsive Design

Assess the designs for their intended devices.

For MAM, review narrow mobile screens, vertical scrolling, safe areas, text readability, touch targets, keyboard interaction, and bottom navigation.

For WAM, assess desktop, laptop, tablet, and mobile layouts where those variants or relevant constraints are accessible.

Review how sidebars, navigation, tables, dashboards, forms, maps, and dialogs adapt at different widths.

Do not infer responsive behavior solely from a desktop frame. Inspect alternate frames or actual layout constraints when accessible. Mark unverified responsive behavior and recommend the specific viewport checks needed.

## 8. Accessibility and Emergency Usability

Evaluate common accessibility and usability concerns, including:
- Text contrast and readability.
- Action labels and instructions.
- Touch-target sizing.
- Form labels and validation feedback.
- Error prevention and recovery.
- Focus and keyboard-use considerations for web screens.
- Information conveyed only by color.
- Clear feedback after user actions.
- Usability for people who are stressed, unfamiliar with the application, or using smaller devices.

Pay particular attention to SOS and reporting flows. Flag unclear emergency actions, potential accidental activation, missing confirmation context, and unclear delivery or status messaging for review.

Do not invent the actual emergency response behavior. Report design concerns and questions requiring clarification.

## 9. Design Completeness and Status

Classify each relevant feature or workflow using:

- **Present:** A relevant design is identifiable.
- **Partially designed:** Some related screens or states exist, but the journey appears incomplete.
- **Missing:** No relevant design was found after inspecting the accessible file.
- **Unclear:** Evidence is insufficient to determine completeness.
- **Needs verification:** The frame exists, but prototype wiring, responsive constraints, or another property could not be confirmed.

A visually polished screen does not prove that its interaction or workflow is complete.

## 10. Audit Report

Produce the report in this order:

### A. Executive Summary
Summarize overall design condition, strengths, major gaps, and the highest-priority concerns.

### B. Inspection Coverage
State the accessible pages, frames, components, and prototype areas actually examined.

### C. Screen and Component Inventory
Organize frames by MAM, WAM, and shared design-system components.

### D. Resident Mobile UX Findings
Document relevant issues, missing states, workflow problems, and recommendations.

### E. Admin Web UX Findings
Document layout, navigation, workflow, and administrative usability concerns.

### F. Navigation and User-Flow Maps
Describe the major workflows in screen order, identifying verified and inferred connections.

### G. Design System Findings
Report inconsistencies in color, typography, spacing, components, icons, and content.

### H. Responsive Design and Accessibility
Separate observed findings from unverified behavior and proposed tests.

### I. Design Gap Register
For each issue, include:
- Unique ID.
- Priority: P0, P1, P2, or P3.
- Application mode.
- Affected frame or component.
- Observed condition and supporting evidence.
- Why it matters.
- Recommended change.
- Dependencies or questions requiring clarification.

Use P0 for potentially unsafe emergency interaction or a critical user-flow issue; P1 for major workflow gaps; P2 for meaningful usability or consistency issues; and P3 for minor polish.

### J. Design-Readiness Checklist
Identify screens and flows that appear ready for development, those needing clarification, and those requiring additional design work.

### K. Prioritized Design Improvement Plan
Group recommendations into critical workflow concerns, navigation and completeness, shared design system, responsive design and accessibility, and visual polish.

For each improvement, provide clear acceptance criteria.

## Strict Constraints

- This audit is read-only.
- Do not edit, move, rename, delete, or create Figma content.
- Do not modify existing components or prototype connections.
- Do not invent screen names, components, or prototype behavior.
- Do not claim interaction or responsive behavior has been verified when it has not.
- Distinguish observations, inferences, and recommendations.
- Reference concrete frame and component names.
- If a tool limit prevents complete inspection, document the remaining coverage and limitations.

Start by identifying the connected file and its accessible structure. Complete the screen inventory and audit before presenting the prioritized recommendations.
